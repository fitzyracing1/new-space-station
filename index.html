A new commercial crewed space station for life after the ISS

A four-person, permanently crewed laboratory, factory and home in low Earth orbit. It is built from five modules on rockets that fly today, and it is designed in the open. Read the design, then tell us what you'd change.

The modules

The station uses a core module with a multi-port node, the pattern proven by Tiangong, built from 4.4 m rigid aluminium modules that fit today's 5 m fairings.

M1

Core-Node Module

The station's brain and hub: avionics, guidance, control-moment gyros, chemical and Hall-effect propulsion, power distribution, the first two solar wings, comms, and a 6-port node. It is also the robotic arm base.

4.4 m × 14 m · ~26.6 t
M2

Habitat Module

8 crew quarters, galley and wardroom, 2 hygiene stations, exercise, regenerative life support, and a water-walled radiation storm shelter. Crew vehicle docking port.

4.4 m × 14 m · ~21 t
M3

Lab & Manufacturing

12+ payload racks, glovebox, furnace and bioprinting bays, science airlock, an Earth-facing window, and 2 external payload sites.

4.4 m × 14 m · ~19.3 t
M4

Airlock Module

Crew lock and equipment lock with 2 EVA suits, for maintenance and external payload work.

3.0 m × 5 m · ~5.6 t
T1

Power & Thermal Truss

Two more roll-out solar wings on sun-tracking gimbals, deployable radiators, ammonia pump modules and extra batteries. This brings the station to full power.

~4 m × 12 m stowed · ~11.4 t
+

Growth port

A free berthing port for a hospitality module, a third-party module, or an expandable habitat. There are 3 IDSS docking ports for crew and cargo vehicles.

Room to grow

Launch & assembly plan

The station becomes useful early: crew can move in after the second launch. Each module also fits Vulcan, New Glenn and Starship, so the program never depends on a single rocket.

L1 · Month 0 · Falcon Heavy

Core-Node Module

A free-flying, powered, controlled station. Robotic arm checkout.

L2 · Month +2 · Falcon Heavy

Habitat Module

Docks to the node under its own propulsion, then is berthed.

C1 · Month +3 · Crew Dragon

First crew of 4

Initial crewed capability (~23 kW).

L3 · Month +5 · Falcon 9

Power & Thermal Truss

Installed by the arm. Full power and heat rejection.

L4 · Month +7 · Falcon Heavy

Lab & Manufacturing

Berthed at the node. Payload operations begin.

L5 · Month +9 · Falcon 9

Airlock

EVA capability. Full operational capability around month +10 to +12.

Design highlights

  • Regenerative ECLSS from the first crew: ≥95% water recovery rising to ≥98%, and ~50% O₂ recovery. This saves ~6.5 t/yr of upmass.
  • Two-fault tolerance against catastrophic hazards where practical, in line with NASA human-rating practice.
  • Lifeboat rule: docked return capacity is always at least the number of crew on board.
  • Hall-effect electric propulsion for routine drag makeup, with chemical reserve for debris avoidance.
  • ≥1 Gbps payload downlink via optical relay links.
  • 15-year structural design life with replaceable arrays, batteries and ECLSS units.

The NASA proposal angle

NASA plans to buy services from commercially owned stations to keep U.S. astronauts continuously in low Earth orbit after the ISS. Its Commercial LEO Destination Contract (CLDC) Final RFP (solicitation 80JSC027R0004) was released on 9 Oct 2026, and proposals are due 8 Dec 2026.

Where this concept fits

  • NASA asks for a station that supports at least 4 crew, including 2 government astronauts, with continuous presence (no more than a 14-day gap between crews). This design is sized for 4 permanent crew plus handover overlap.
  • NASA allows a phased path from crew-tended (Mission A) to continuous crew (Mission B) to Standard Service. The station's launch-by-launch build-up maps onto that path.
  • The first 4 missions must use already-certified crew and cargo vehicles. The baseline is Crew Dragon and Cargo Dragon on IDSS ports.
  • U.S. launch vehicles throughout: Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy, with Vulcan and New Glenn as alternates.

Open challenges, where we want your input

  • Schedule: the baseline first launch is ~2033, but NASA wants a station before the ISS retires at the end of 2030. Can we compress the schedule, or should we partner?
  • Science accommodation: mapping the lab to NASA's utilization minimums (locker locations, conditioned stowage, vacuum, workbench, glovebox).
  • Crew capacity: should there be more than 4 permanent berths, so commercial users still fly when NASA buys several seats?
  • Certification basis: building a compliance matrix against NASA's commercial LEO requirements and standards.
